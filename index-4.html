What is electricity? Voltage, current and resistance simply explained

Published: 3 October 2026

Electricity is the flow of electric charge, usually electrons moving through a conductor. Three ideas explain most circuits.

Voltage (V) is the push that moves charge, like water pressure in a pipe. Current (I), measured in amperes, is how much charge flows each second, like the amount of water passing. Resistance (R), measured in ohms, is how much the material resists the flow, like a narrow pipe.

They connect through Ohm's Law: V = I × R. If a 12 V battery drives a 4 Ω resistor, the current is 12 ÷ 4 = 3 A. Master this one equation and you can already analyse simple circuits.

What is artificial intelligence? A simple explanation

Published: 3 October 2026

Artificial intelligence (AI) is software that learns patterns from large amounts of data and uses them to make predictions or produce answers, such as recognising speech, translating text or suggesting what to read next.

It is a tool, not magic. It can be wrong, so it is wise to check important answers. For students, AI can explain ideas in new ways and help practise problems, but real understanding still comes from thinking and working through the material yourself.

Series vs parallel circuits: what is the difference?

Published: 3 October 2026

In a series circuit, components are connected in one line. The same current flows through all of them, the voltages add up, and if one component breaks, everything stops.

In a parallel circuit, each component has its own branch. Every branch gets the same voltage, the currents add up, and if one branch fails the others keep working. This is why the lights and sockets in a house are wired in parallel.

How does the internet work? A simple explanation

Published: 3 October 2026

The internet is a huge network of connected networks. When you open a website, your device sends a request that travels through routers until it reaches a server that stores the page.

Data is broken into small pieces called packets, sent separately and put back together on your device. A system called DNS works like a phone book, turning a name such as a website address into the number of the right server.

How to solve physics problems step by step

Published: 3 October 2026

1. Write down what is given and what you must find. 2. Draw a quick sketch. 3. Choose the formula that connects them. 4. Check that all units match, for example metres and seconds. 5. Substitute the numbers and calculate. 6. Ask whether the answer makes sense.

Following the same steps every time makes hard problems feel manageable, and it also helps you find where you made a mistake.

Study tips

1. Start on day one

Begin at the start of the year, not the last month. Small daily effort beats last-minute panic.

2. Same time every day

A fixed study time turns effort into a habit, so you rely less on motivation.

3. Study with friends

A group in a quiet place keeps you committed. Explain topics to each other.

4. Use past papers early

They show the exam style and your weak topics while there is still time to fix them.

5. Ask when stuck

Teachers and classmates can clear in minutes what takes hours alone.

6. Rest and review

Sleep well and review notes briefly each week so knowledge stays fresh.

Quick notes

Electricity

Ohm's Law: V = I × R
Power: P = V × I (also P = I²R and P = V²/R)
Energy: E = P × t
Resistors in series: R = R₁ + R₂ + …
Resistors in parallel: 1/R = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + …

Physics

Speed: v = d / t
Acceleration: a = (v − u) / t
Force: F = m × a
Weight: W = m × g (g ≈ 9.8 m/s²)
Work: W = F × d

Maths

Pythagoras: a² + b² = c²
Quadratic formula: x = (−b ± √(b² − 4ac)) / 2a
Area of a circle: A = πr²
Circumference: C = 2πr

Exam day checklist

Sleep early the night before · Prepare pens, ID and required items · Arrive early · Read every question carefully · Start with the questions you know · Check your answers before time ends.
